\begin{abstract}
Most dropout-prediction studies on the public UCI \emph{Predict Students' Dropout and Academic Success} dataset ($N=4{,}424$) either drop the third outcome, \emph{Enrolled}, or treat it as a class whose fate is unknown. In this dataset the label is assigned at the end of the normal duration of the degree, so Enrolled students are those who neither graduated on time nor left: they are delayed. We compare eight classifiers in a binary setting (Dropout vs.\ Graduate) and in the three-class setting at three points in time (enrolment, end of the first semester, end of the second semester), using repeated stratified cross-validation and corrected resampled $t$-tests. In the binary setting, six of the seven models compared with the best one are statistically indistinguishable from it (best macro F1 \bestbinaryF). In the three-class setting, macro F1 falls to \bestmulticlassF{} (XGBoost), and most of the drop comes from the delayed group: F1 is \multiclassXGBoostfdropout{} for Dropout, \multiclassXGBoostfgraduate{} for Graduate and \multiclassXGBoostfenrolled{} for Enrolled. Dropout itself remains well ranked inside the three-class model (AUC \multiclassXGBoostaucdropout), and its predicted probabilities are well calibrated (expected calibration error \eceXGBoost). With first-semester records only, the model keeps \retainsem\% of its macro F1; with enrolment data only, \retainenrol\%. Selecting features inside the cross-validation loop did not improve on the full feature set. Subgroup analysis shows that the model misses more of the dropouts in groups where dropout is rare, for example scholarship holders (recall \subScholarshipYesTpr{} vs.\ \subScholarshipNoTpr) and students who enrolled at 20 or younger (\subAgeatenrolmentletwentyTpr{} vs.\ \subAgeatenrolmentgttwentyfiveTpr{} for those over 25). Code and all reported numbers are generated from a public repository.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} student dropout, delayed completion, early warning systems, educational data mining, model evaluation, calibration, algorithmic fairness

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Universities that want to reduce dropout increasingly use predictive models to decide which students should receive advising or financial support \citep{arnold2012,jayaprakash2014}. Educational data mining has produced a large literature on such models \citep{romero2010,romero2020,namoun2021,mduma2019}, and since its release in 2021 the UCI \emph{Predict Students' Dropout and Academic Success} dataset \citep{realinho2021uci,realinho2022} has become a common benchmark for them \citep{martins2021,villar2024,gu2026,vo2026}.

Three choices in how this benchmark is used affect what the reported numbers mean.

\emph{The outcome definition.} The dataset has three labels: Dropout, Graduate and Enrolled. Many studies remove the Enrolled students and report binary results, and those that keep them often describe Enrolled as an unresolved outcome. According to the dataset authors, however, the label is assigned at the end of the normal duration of the course \citep{realinho2022}. An Enrolled student is therefore one who is still registered after the time in which the degree should have been completed. This is a delay, which matters to an institution in its own right, and it is observed rather than missing.

\emph{The prediction point.} A model that uses second-semester grades cannot act before the end of the first year. The value of an early warning system depends on what can be predicted earlier, ideally at enrolment.

\emph{The evaluation protocol.} Results on this dataset are often reported for a single train/test split, and model rankings are read from differences in the third decimal place. With about 900 test students, such differences are usually within sampling noise.

This paper revisits the benchmark with these three points in mind. We ask:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How much do established classifiers differ on binary dropout prediction once sampling variation is accounted for?
  \item[RQ2] What happens when delayed (Enrolled) students are kept as a third class, and which part of the problem becomes harder?
  \item[RQ3] How much predictive performance is available at enrolment and after the first semester?
  \item[RQ4] Are the predicted dropout probabilities calibrated, and do error rates differ between student subgroups?
\end{description}

Our contributions are: (i) a re-reading of the Enrolled label as delayed completion, with per-class results showing that the drop in macro F1 in the three-class setting comes from this class rather than from dropout detection; (ii) a comparison of eight classifiers under repeated cross-validation with corrected significance tests, which shows that most published rankings on this dataset are not statistically supported; (iii) a staged analysis from enrolment to the end of the first year; (iv) calibration and subgroup error analyses, including the finding that recall is lowest in groups with low base rates of dropout; and (v) a repository in which every number in the paper is produced by code.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Early warning systems.} \citet{macfadyen2010} showed that learning-management-system activity could identify students at risk of failing a course. Purdue's Course Signals \citep{arnold2012} and the Open Academic Analytics Initiative \citep{jayaprakash2014} put such predictions into practice across institutions and combined them with outreach to students. \citet{howard2018} compared eight prediction methods on undergraduate data and found the middle of the first semester to be a good compromise between timeliness and accuracy. \citet{akcapinar2019} identified at-risk students from online interaction data within the first weeks of a course. \citet{kemper2020} predicted dropout at a German technical university from examination records alone, using logistic regression and decision trees.

\paragraph{Studies on this dataset.} \citet{realinho2022} describe the data and its collection at the Polytechnic Institute of Portalegre, Portugal; \citet{martins2021} report early predictive models on the same data. \citet{villar2024} compared tree ensembles including XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost, with SMOTE oversampling and Optuna tuning. \citet{gu2026} reduced the features to 20 with correlation filtering and recursive feature elimination and reported an AUC of 0.891 for gradient boosting, close to the values we obtain with all features. \citet{vo2026} compared ten algorithms on a three-class problem with the same outcome labels and reported a best macro F1 of 0.708 (LightGBM) and a best one-vs-rest AUC of 0.892 (random forest).

\paragraph{Explanations, imbalance and fairness.} SHAP values \citep{lundberg2017,lundberg2020} are now widely used to explain dropout models \citep{nagy2024,krueger2023}. For imbalanced classes, \citet{wongvorachan2023} and \citet{mduma2023} compared resampling methods and found that the best choice depends on the data. \citet{baker2022} review algorithmic bias in education; \citet{gardner2019} propose slicing analysis to compare model performance across groups, and \citet{hu2020} propose fairness-aware training for at-risk prediction. \citet{kizilcec2022} discuss how fairness definitions translate to educational decisions.

\section{Data and outcome definition}
\label{sec:data}

The dataset \citep{realinho2021uci} contains 4{,}424 students enrolled between 2008/09 and 2018/19 in undergraduate degrees at one Portuguese polytechnic institute, with 36 features and no missing values. The features cover demographics and family background (e.g.\ age at enrolment, gender, parents' qualifications and occupations), the application (mode, order, course, admission grade), financial status (scholarship, debtor, tuition fees up to date), national economic indicators for the year of enrolment, and six curricular variables for each of the first two semesters (units credited, enrolled, evaluated, approved and without evaluation, and the mean grade).

The outcome is recorded at the end of the normal duration of the course \citep{realinho2022}: 2{,}209 students (49.9\%) graduated, 1{,}421 (32.1\%) dropped out, and 794 (17.9\%) were still enrolled. We refer to the last group as \emph{delayed}. Their final outcome is not in the data, so the class mixes students who will later graduate and students who will later leave. That makes it harder to predict, but it is a real state that an institution may want to act on, since delayed completion has its own costs for students and institutions.

\paragraph{Settings.} We use four settings.
\textbf{Binary}: Dropout vs.\ Graduate after removing delayed students ($n=3{,}630$, 36 features).
\textbf{Three-class}: all students and all features ($n=4{,}424$).
\textbf{First semester}: three-class, without the six second-semester variables (30 features).
\textbf{Enrolment}: three-class, without either semester's curricular variables (24 features), i.e.\ information available when the student starts.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{Models.} We compare logistic regression, a decision tree, a random forest, an RBF-kernel support vector machine, gradient boosting (scikit-learn), XGBoost \citep{chen2016}, LightGBM \citep{ke2017} and a multilayer perceptron (three hidden layers of 256, 128 and 64 units, early stopping). Hyperparameters are fixed in advance and listed in the repository; no model was tuned on the evaluation folds. For logistic regression, the SVM and the MLP, the nine nominal variables coded as integers (e.g.\ course, application mode, parents' occupations) are one-hot encoded, with levels seen fewer than 20 times in the training fold pooled, and the remaining variables are standardised. Tree-based models receive the raw values. All preprocessing is fitted on training folds only. We use scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Each model is evaluated with stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated five times with different shuffles, giving 25 test folds per model and setting \citep{kohavi1995}. We report the mean and standard deviation over folds of accuracy, macro-averaged precision, recall and F1, and the macro one-vs-rest AUC. In the three-class settings we also report class-wise F1 and two dropout-specific measures computed from the predicted probability of the Dropout class: the AUC for Dropout vs.\ all other students and the Brier score. Predictions use the class with the highest probability.

\paragraph{Comparing models.} Fold scores from repeated cross-validation are not independent, because training sets overlap, so a naive paired $t$-test overstates significance. We compare every model with the best one in each setting using the corrected resampled $t$-test of \citet{nadeau2003}, which inflates the variance by a factor that accounts for the overlap, and we treat $p\ge 0.05$ as ``not distinguishable''. We report these tests to show which differences are robust; we do not correct for multiple comparisons, which would only enlarge the set of tied models.

\paragraph{Calibration.} An early warning system usually ranks or thresholds probabilities, so they should mean what they say \citep{niculescu2005}. Using out-of-fold predictions from the first cross-validation repeat (one prediction per student), we plot reliability curves for the predicted dropout probability with ten quantile bins and report the expected calibration error (ECE) and the Brier score \citep{guo2017}.

\paragraph{Subgroups.} With the same out-of-fold predictions from the best three-class model, we compare gender, scholarship status and age at enrolment ($\le 20$, 21--25, $>25$). For each group we report dropout recall (the share of actual dropouts predicted as Dropout), the false-alert rate (the share of other students predicted as Dropout) and macro F1, with 95\% bootstrap intervals from 1{,}000 resamples within the group. Gender and scholarship status are themselves model inputs; we analyse errors, not the causal role of these attributes.

\paragraph{Feature selection.} To test whether a smaller feature set suffices, we place ANOVA-$F$ feature selection inside the model pipeline, so that features are chosen on the training part of each fold, and evaluate $k\in\{5,10,15,20,25,30,36\}$ with 5-fold cross-validation repeated three times.

\paragraph{Explanations.} We compute SHAP values \citep{lundberg2017} with the TreeExplainer \citep{lundberg2020} for the XGBoost three-class model trained on a stratified 80\% split and explained on the remaining 20\% (macro F1 on that split: \shapXgbF). SHAP values are in log-odds units for each class. Because XGBoost is also the best model in the main comparison, no proxy model is needed.

\subsection{Binary setting (RQ1)}

Table~\ref{tab:binary} shows the binary results. LightGBM has the highest mean macro F1 (\binaryLightGBMf) and XGBoost the highest AUC (\binaryXGBoostauc), but the corrected tests find no difference in macro F1 between the best model and \nTiedbinaryf{} of the other seven, including logistic regression (\binaryLogisticRegressionf). Only the single decision tree is clearly worse (\binaryDecisionTreef). Once the nominal codes are one-hot encoded, a linear model matches the boosted ensembles here. Rankings among the top models on a single split should therefore not be read as evidence that one algorithm is better.

\subsection{Three-class setting (RQ2)}

Table~\ref{tab:multi} and Figure~\ref{fig:overview} show the three-class results. XGBoost has the highest macro F1 (\multiclassXGBoostf) and AUC (\multiclassXGBoostauc). In macro F1 it is not distinguishable from LightGBM, gradient boosting, logistic regression or the MLP; it is better than the random forest, the SVM and the decision tree. Its AUC advantage over every other model is significant but small (0.02 or less, except over the decision tree).

Averaged over the eight models, moving from the binary to the three-class setting lowers macro F1 by \gapf{} (\gapRelf\% of the binary value), accuracy by \gapaccuracy{} and macro AUC by \gapauc. The class-wise scores show where this comes from. For XGBoost, F1 is \multiclassXGBoostfdropout{} for Dropout and \multiclassXGBoostfgraduate{} for Graduate but only \multiclassXGBoostfenrolled{} for Enrolled. The confusion matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}) shows that \cmEnrolledCorrect\% of delayed students are recognised, \cmEnrolledAsGrad\% are predicted to graduate and \cmEnrolledAsDrop\% to drop out. Delayed students sit between the two terminal groups in feature space, and as noted in Section~\ref{sec:data} the class itself mixes future graduates and future dropouts.

Dropout detection, the task most institutions care about, is much less affected. The probability that the three-class XGBoost model assigns to Dropout ranks dropouts against all other students with an AUC of \multiclassXGBoostaucdropout. This is lower than the binary AUC (\binaryXGBoostaucdropout), but the two numbers answer different questions: the binary model never has to distinguish dropouts from delayed students, who are the hardest cases. The binary figure is therefore an optimistic estimate of how a deployed model would perform on a full cohort.

\subsection{Prediction at enrolment and after the first semester (RQ3)}

Table~\ref{tab:stages} and Figure~\ref{fig:perclass} follow the three-class problem through time. With enrolment data only, the best macro F1 is \stageEnrolF{} and the Dropout AUC \stageEnrolAucDrop. Adding first-semester records raises these to \stageSemoneF{} and \stageSemoneAucDrop; adding the second semester gives \stageFullF{} and \stageFullAucDrop. XGBoost keeps \retainsem\% of its full macro F1 with first-semester data and \retainenrol\% with enrolment data only. Most of the information arrives with the first semester. The delayed class benefits most from the second semester (F1 from \stageSemoneFenrolled{} to \stageFullFenrolled), which fits the idea that delay builds up through units that are not passed in the second semester. A first-semester model is therefore a reasonable basis for intervention one semester earlier, while a model at enrolment ranks dropout risk moderately well (AUC \stageEnrolAucDrop) but should only be used to direct low-cost support.

\subsection{Subgroups (RQ4)}

Table~\ref{tab:subgroups} and Figure~\ref{fig:subgroups} report errors by group. The clearest pattern concerns recall. Among scholarship holders, whose dropout rate is \subScholarshipYesRate, the model identifies \subScholarshipYesTpr{} of the dropouts, against \subScholarshipNoTpr{} among other students (dropout rate \subScholarshipNoRate). Students who enrolled at 20 or younger (dropout rate \subAgeatenrolmentletwentyRate) have a recall of \subAgeatenrolmentletwentyTpr, compared with \subAgeatenrolmentgttwentyfiveTpr{} for students older than 25 (dropout rate \subAgeatenrolmentgttwentyfiveRate). Male students, whose dropout rate is higher (\subGenderMaleRate{} vs.\ \subGenderFemaleRate), receive more false alerts (\subGenderMaleFpr{} vs.\ \subGenderFemaleFpr) and also have higher recall.

In each case the model is more sensitive in the group where dropout is common and misses more dropouts where it is rare. This is what one expects when group membership, or variables correlated with it, carries part of the signal. In practice it means that an alert list built from a single threshold under-represents dropouts from low-risk groups, such as young full-time students and scholarship holders. Group-specific thresholds or a review of the missed cases are possible remedies; which is appropriate depends on the institution's goals \citep{kizilcec2022,baker2022}.

\subsection{Features}

\paragraph{Feature selection.} With selection performed inside cross-validation, no reduced feature set improved on all 36 features (Figure~\ref{fig:fs}; macro F1 \fsFull). Keeping 30 features (\fsKthreezero) stays within one standard deviation of the full model; keeping 20 gives \fsKtwozero{} and keeping 10 gives \fsKonezero. A selection step evaluated on the test split can make small feature sets look as good as the full set; this does not hold up when the selection is part of the training procedure.

\paragraph{SHAP.} Figure~\ref{fig:shap} shows mean absolute SHAP values and the distribution for the Dropout class. The number of second-semester units approved dominates (mean $|$SHAP$|$ \shapTopOneVal{} averaged over classes), followed by first-semester units approved (\shapTopTwoVal) and whether tuition fees are up to date (\shapTopThreeVal). For the Dropout class, few approved units and unpaid fees both push predictions towards dropout; older age at enrolment does the same. For the Enrolled class, the number of assessments taken in each semester carries almost as much weight as the number of units approved, which is not the case for the other two classes. These are associations learned by the model. They identify where students who drop out differ, not why they drop out; fee arrears, for example, may be a consequence of a decision to leave as much as a cause.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the Enrolled class is.} Reading Enrolled as delayed completion changes the interpretation of the three-class problem. The low F1 for this class is not a sign that the models fail on unresolved futures; it shows that delay is hard to separate from both dropout and graduation with the available variables, partly because the class contains students on both paths. For institutions this suggests two separate targets: dropout, which the models detect well, and delay, which they detect poorly and which may need more frequent data (for example, term-by-term progress) than two semester summaries provide.

\paragraph{Which model.} Five of the eight models are statistically tied in three-class macro F1, and seven of the eight in the binary setting. Logistic regression is among the tied models in both, has calibrated probabilities and is easy to explain to advisers. Boosted trees give small but consistent AUC gains. A choice between them can reasonably be made on interpretability and maintenance rather than on accuracy. This agrees with the general finding that tree ensembles are strong on tabular data \citep{grinsztajn2022}, but here the margin over a well-specified linear model is small.

\paragraph{Reporting.} Three practices would make results on this benchmark easier to compare: reporting class-wise scores alongside macro averages, using repeated cross-validation with corrected tests instead of one split, and performing feature selection and resampling inside the training folds.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The data come from one institution and cover cohorts up to 2018/19, so the results may not transfer to other institutions or to later cohorts; external validation is the most important next step. Hyperparameters were fixed rather than tuned; nested tuning might change the ranking within the group of tied models but is unlikely to remove the gap for the delayed class. We did not use resampling methods such as SMOTE; they can raise recall for the delayed class at the cost of precision and calibration. The subgroup analysis uses three attributes and does not consider intersections. Finally, the predictors are semester summaries; finer-grained data could help with the delayed class in particular.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On the UCI dropout benchmark, binary results overstate how well a deployed model separates students, most model rankings are within noise, and the main difficulty of the three-class problem lies in identifying delayed students rather than dropouts. Dropout itself can be ranked well after one semester (AUC \stageSemoneAucDrop) with calibrated probabilities. Recall is lower in groups where dropout is rare, which institutions should take into account when they turn scores into alert lists.
