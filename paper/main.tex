\begin{abstract}
Most dropout-prediction studies on the public UCI \emph{Predict Students' Dropout and Academic Success} dataset ($N=4{,}424$) drop the third outcome, \emph{Enrolled}, or treat it as an unknown fate. The label is assigned at the end of the normal course duration, so Enrolled students are delayed, having neither graduated on time nor left. We compare eight classifiers in a binary and a three-class setting at three prediction points, using repeated cross-validation, corrected $t$-tests and nested tuning. Most models are statistically tied. In the three-class setting, macro F1 falls to \bestmulticlassF{}, mainly because of the delayed class (F1 \multiclassXGBoostfenrolled{}, against \multiclassXGBoostfdropout{} for Dropout and \multiclassXGBoostfgraduate{} for Graduate), while dropout itself is ranked well (AUC \multiclassXGBoostaucdropout) with calibrated probabilities. After one semester, the top 20\% of students by predicted risk include \alertSemoneRPctTwenty{} of eventual dropouts, and \alertSemonePPctTwenty{} of the listed students drop out. Recall is lower where dropout is rare: \thrScholarshipYesSingleRecall{} for scholarship holders against \thrScholarshipNoSingleRecall{} for others, and group-specific thresholds raise it to \thrScholarshipYesGroupRecall{} while precision moves from \thrScholarshipAllSinglePrecision{} to \thrScholarshipAllGroupPrecision. We repeat the dropout analysis on \upvN{} first-year students at a Spanish university that defines dropout differently. Model ties, calibration and the recall gap with its correction replicate there, but the better transfer of logistic regression to unseen programmes seen at UCI does not. A model trained on an earlier cohort loses accuracy on a later one (first-semester AUC \upvSemoneXgbAuc{} in cross-validation, \upvTaSemoneXgbAuc{} on the next cohort), and learning-platform activity adds little once grades are known. Results at enrolment depend on two fee-status variables of undocumented timing. All numbers are generated from a public repository.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} student dropout, delayed completion, early warning systems, educational data mining, model evaluation, calibration, algorithmic fairness, external validation

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Universities that want to reduce dropout increasingly use predictive models to decide which students should receive advising or financial support \citep{arnold2012,jayaprakash2014}. Educational data mining has produced a large literature on such models \citep{romero2010,romero2020,namoun2021,mduma2019}, and since its release in 2021 the UCI \emph{Predict Students' Dropout and Academic Success} dataset \citep{realinho2021uci,realinho2022} has become a common benchmark for them \citep{martins2021,villar2024,gu2026,vo2026}.

Several choices in how this benchmark is used, and one property of the data, affect what the reported numbers mean.

\emph{The outcome definition.} The dataset has three labels: Dropout, Graduate and Enrolled. Many studies remove the Enrolled students and report binary results, and those that keep them often describe Enrolled as an unresolved outcome. According to the dataset authors, however, the label is assigned at the end of the normal duration of the course \citep{realinho2022}. An Enrolled student is one who is still registered after the degree should have been completed. This is a delay that matters to an institution in its own right, and the data record it.

\emph{The prediction point.} A model that uses second-semester grades cannot act before the end of the first year. The value of an early warning system depends on what can be predicted earlier, ideally at enrolment.

\emph{The evaluation protocol.} Results on this dataset are often reported for a single train/test split, and model rankings are read from differences in the third decimal place. With about 900 test students, such differences are usually within sampling noise.

\emph{The data source.} All of these students come from one polytechnic institute. A result found there may depend on how that institute records its data, and Section~\ref{sec:transfer} finds one programme where it does. Findings about dropout can be checked at another university, and we do so with open data from Spain that define dropout differently.

This paper revisits the benchmark with these four points in mind. We ask:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How much do established classifiers differ on binary dropout prediction once sampling variation is accounted for?
  \item[RQ2] What happens when delayed (Enrolled) students are kept as a third class, and which part of the problem becomes harder?
  \item[RQ3] How much predictive performance is available at enrolment and after the first semester?
  \item[RQ4] Are the predicted dropout probabilities calibrated, and do error rates differ between student subgroups?
  \item[RQ5] How useful are the scores when an institution can contact only a fixed share of students, and what does it cost to give every subgroup the same dropout recall?
  \item[RQ6] Which of the dropout findings hold at a second university, with its own definition of dropout, other variables and several entry cohorts?
\end{description}

Our contributions are: (i) a re-reading of the Enrolled label as delayed completion, with per-class results showing that the drop in macro F1 in the three-class setting comes from this class rather than from dropout detection; (ii) a comparison of eight classifiers under repeated cross-validation with corrected significance tests, which shows that most published rankings on this dataset are not statistically supported; (iii) a staged analysis from enrolment to the end of the first year; (iv) calibration and subgroup error analyses, including the finding that recall is lowest in groups with low base rates of dropout; (v) a decision-oriented evaluation of fixed-size alert lists and of group-specific thresholds; (vi) checks of threats to validity: transfer to degree programmes the model has not seen, fee-status variables of uncertain timing, and untuned hyperparameters; (vii) a replication of the dropout results at the Universitat Polit\`ecnica de Val\`encia, with tests on later entry cohorts and unseen degrees; and (viii) a repository in which every number in the paper is produced by code.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Early warning systems.} \citet{macfadyen2010} showed that learning-management-system activity could identify students at risk of failing a course. Purdue's Course Signals \citep{arnold2012} and the Open Academic Analytics Initiative \citep{jayaprakash2014} put such predictions into practice across institutions and combined them with outreach to students. \citet{howard2018} compared eight prediction methods on undergraduate data and found the middle of the first semester to be a good compromise between timeliness and accuracy. \citet{akcapinar2019} identified at-risk students from online interaction data within the first weeks of a course. \citet{kemper2020} predicted dropout at a German technical university from examination records alone, using logistic regression and decision trees.

\paragraph{Studies on this dataset.} \citet{realinho2022} describe the data and its collection at the Polytechnic Institute of Portalegre, Portugal; \citet{martins2021} report early predictive models on the same data. \citet{villar2024} compared tree ensembles including XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost, with SMOTE oversampling and Optuna tuning. \citet{gu2026} reduced the features to 20 with correlation filtering and recursive feature elimination and reported an AUC of 0.891 for gradient boosting, close to the values we obtain with all features. \citet{vo2026} compared ten algorithms on a three-class problem with the same outcome labels and reported a best macro F1 of 0.708 (LightGBM) and a best one-vs-rest AUC of 0.892 (random forest).

\paragraph{Explanations, imbalance and fairness.} SHAP values \citep{lundberg2017,lundberg2020} are now widely used to explain dropout models \citep{nagy2024,krueger2023}. For imbalanced classes, \citet{wongvorachan2023} and \citet{mduma2023} compared resampling methods and found that the best choice depends on the data. \citet{baker2022} review algorithmic bias in education; \citet{gardner2019} propose slicing analysis to compare model performance across groups, and \citet{hu2020} propose fairness-aware training for at-risk prediction. \citet{kizilcec2022} discuss how fairness definitions translate to educational decisions.

\section{Data and outcome definition}
\label{sec:data}

The dataset \citep{realinho2021uci} contains 4{,}424 students enrolled between 2008/09 and 2018/19 in undergraduate degrees at one Portuguese polytechnic institute, with 36 features and no missing values. The features cover demographics and family background (e.g.\ age at enrolment, gender, parents' qualifications and occupations), the application (mode, order, course, admission grade), financial status (scholarship, debtor, tuition fees up to date), national economic indicators for the year of enrolment, and six curricular variables for each of the first two semesters (units credited, enrolled, evaluated, approved and without evaluation, and the mean grade).

The outcome is recorded at the end of the normal duration of the course \citep{realinho2022}: 2{,}209 students (49.9\%) graduated, 1{,}421 (32.1\%) dropped out, and 794 (17.9\%) were still enrolled. We refer to the last group as \emph{delayed}. Their final outcome is not in the data, so the class mixes students who will later graduate and students who will later leave. That makes it harder to predict, but it is a real state that an institution may want to act on, since delayed completion has its own costs for students and institutions.

\paragraph{Settings.}
\textbf{Binary} (Dropout vs.\ Graduate): delayed students are removed ($n=3{,}630$, 36 features).
\textbf{Three-class}: all students and all features ($n=4{,}424$).
\textbf{First semester}: three-class, without the six second-semester variables (30 features).
\textbf{Enrolment}: three-class, without either semester's curricular variables (24 features), i.e.\ information available when the student starts.

\paragraph{Second university.} For the replication we use data from the Universitat Polit\`ecnica de Val\`encia (UPV), Spain \citep{igualde2025}. The records cover the academic years starting in 2018, 2021 and 2022 (the pandemic years are left out), with one row per student and course. We keep students in their first year who entered the university that year: \upvN{} students, of whom \upvNEighteen{} entered in 2018, \upvNTwentyone{} in 2021 and \upvNTwentytwo{} in 2022. Dropout is the university's own flag. A student counts as a dropout after two consecutive years without enrolment while credits are still outstanding, and moving to another degree at UPV does not count. \upvRatePct\% of the students dropped out (\upvRateEighteen, \upvRateTwentyone{} and \upvRateTwentytwo{} by entry year), far below the 32\% at UCI. The data have no graduation or delay label, so the task is dropout against all other students. At UCI the matching quantity is the Dropout AUC of the three-class model, since the binary setting leaves out the delayed students.

The UPV variables differ from those at UCI. Known at enrolment are the access route, two admission grades, how highly the student ranked the degree when applying, enrolled credits per semester, part-time status, whether the student moved to study, parents' education, campus and degree. Results come as credits passed in each semester and the mean course grade. The data also hold monthly activity in the university's learning platform (events, visits, days and minutes logged in, submissions and use of course resources), which we sum over each student's courses, and the number of days the student used the campus Wi-Fi, which was not recorded in 2018. Gender, age and scholarship status are not in the data.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\paragraph{Models.} We compare logistic regression, a decision tree, a random forest, an RBF-kernel support vector machine, gradient boosting (scikit-learn), XGBoost \citep{chen2016}, LightGBM \citep{ke2017} and a multilayer perceptron (three hidden layers of 256, 128 and 64 units, early stopping). Hyperparameters are fixed in advance and listed in the repository; no model was tuned on the evaluation folds. For logistic regression, the SVM and the MLP, the nine nominal variables coded as integers (e.g.\ course, application mode, parents' occupations) are one-hot encoded, with levels seen fewer than 20 times in the training fold pooled, and the remaining variables are standardised. Tree-based models receive the raw values. All preprocessing is fitted on training folds only. We use scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011}.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} Each model is evaluated with stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated five times with different shuffles, giving 25 test folds per model and setting \citep{kohavi1995}. We report the mean and standard deviation over folds of accuracy, macro-averaged precision, recall and F1, and the macro one-vs-rest AUC. In the three-class settings we also report class-wise F1 and two dropout-specific measures computed from the predicted probability of the Dropout class: the AUC for Dropout vs.\ all other students and the Brier score. Predictions use the class with the highest probability.

\paragraph{Comparing models.} Fold scores from repeated cross-validation are not independent, because training sets overlap, so a naive paired $t$-test overstates significance. We compare every model with the best one in each setting using the corrected resampled $t$-test of \citet{nadeau2003}, which inflates the variance by a factor that accounts for the overlap, and we treat $p\ge 0.05$ as ``not distinguishable''. We report these tests to show which differences hold up, and we do not correct for multiple comparisons, which would only enlarge the set of tied models.

\paragraph{Calibration.} An early warning system usually ranks or thresholds probabilities, so they should mean what they say \citep{niculescu2005}. Using out-of-fold predictions from the first cross-validation repeat (one prediction per student), we plot reliability curves for the predicted dropout probability with ten quantile bins and report the expected calibration error (ECE) and the Brier score \citep{guo2017}.

\paragraph{Subgroups.} With the same out-of-fold predictions from the best three-class model, we compare gender, scholarship status and age at enrolment ($\le 20$, 21--25, $>25$). For each group we report dropout recall (the share of actual dropouts predicted as Dropout), the false-alert rate (the share of other students predicted as Dropout) and macro F1, with 95\% bootstrap intervals from 1{,}000 resamples within the group. Gender and scholarship status are themselves model inputs, and the analysis concerns the model's errors in these groups.

\paragraph{Alert lists.} Advising capacity is usually fixed in advance, so we also evaluate the scores as ranked lists. Students are sorted by their out-of-fold XGBoost probability of dropout, and the top $c\%$ of the cohort are flagged for $c$ between 5 and 50. We report precision (the share of flagged students who drop out) and reach (the share of all dropouts who are flagged), with 95\% bootstrap intervals, at each of the three prediction points.

\paragraph{Group-specific thresholds.} Using the scores of the model with both semesters, a single threshold that flags 20\% of the cohort is compared with group-specific thresholds chosen so that, within each group, the same share of dropouts is flagged as under the single threshold overall. To avoid choosing a threshold with the same students it is evaluated on, we cross-fit: students are split into five folds stratified by outcome and group, thresholds are set on four folds and applied to the fifth.

\paragraph{Fee-status variables.} Two inputs, \emph{Debtor} and \emph{Tuition fees up to date}, are among the strongest predictors, and 87\% of students whose fees are not up to date drop out. The data descriptor groups socio-economic attributes with information known at enrolment, but fee status can change during studies and its recording date is not given. If they reflect the situation after a student stopped attending, they partly encode the outcome. We repeat the three-class analysis at each prediction point without them, for XGBoost and logistic regression.

\paragraph{Unseen programmes.} The data come from 17 degree programmes. As a partial substitute for external validation, we hold out each programme in turn, train on the other 16 and predict the held-out one (leave-one-programme-out). The programme code is removed from the features, because a new programme would have an unseen code. The reference is the same model, also without the programme code, evaluated with repeated 5-fold CV over all students. We report the Dropout AUC within each programme, the pooled Dropout AUC and macro F1 over all students, and the gap between the mean predicted and the observed dropout rate per programme. Intervals for the pooled change come from a bootstrap that resamples whole programmes. We do this for XGBoost and logistic regression after the first and second semesters.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter tuning.} To check that fixed hyperparameters do not drive the comparison, we run nested cross-validation for four models in the three-class setting: an outer stratified 5-fold CV repeated twice, and inside each outer training set a randomised search (12 to 20 configurations, 3-fold CV, macro F1). Tuned and fixed versions are compared on the same outer folds with the corrected $t$-test.

\paragraph{Replication at UPV.} The same eight models with the same hyperparameters are fitted at five prediction points: enrolment, after the first semester, the same with learning-platform and Wi-Fi activity up to January, the end of the first year, and the end of the year with activity up to June. Categorical variables are one-hot encoded, with levels seen fewer than 30 times pooled. Missing numeric values are set to the median over all students and flagged with an indicator, a step that does not use the outcome. Cross-validation, the corrected tests, calibration, alert lists and cross-fitted group thresholds follow the procedures above, with folds stratified by outcome and entry year. Gender, age and scholarship are not recorded, so the groups are study mode (full-time or part-time) and access route (the most common route, coded NAP in the data, against all others).

Three further checks have no counterpart at UCI. For later cohorts, a model trained on the 2018 entrants is tested on the 2021 entrants, and a model trained on 2018 and 2021 is tested on 2022. The second split is closer to how a model would be used, but the 2022 labels are the most recent and the most likely to be incomplete, because dropout is confirmed only after two years without enrolment. For unseen degrees, folds are grouped by degree and the degree indicators are removed. Credits passed in the first semester may include resit examinations taken after the semester ends, so as a last check we also fit a first-semester model that has the activity data up to January and no first-semester credits.

\paragraph{Feature selection.} To test whether a smaller feature set suffices, we place ANOVA-$F$ feature selection inside the model pipeline, so that features are chosen on the training part of each fold, and evaluate $k\in\{5,10,15,20,25,30,36\}$ with 5-fold cross-validation repeated three times.

\paragraph{Explanations.} We compute SHAP values \citep{lundberg2017} with the TreeExplainer \citep{lundberg2020} for the XGBoost three-class model trained on a stratified 80\% split and explained on the remaining 20\% (macro F1 \shapXgbF{} on that split). SHAP values are in log-odds units for each class. Because XGBoost is also the best model in the main comparison, no proxy model is needed.

\subsection{Three-class setting (RQ2)}

Table~\ref{tab:multi} and Figure~\ref{fig:overview} show the three-class results. XGBoost has the highest macro F1 (\multiclassXGBoostf) and AUC (\multiclassXGBoostauc). In macro F1 it is not distinguishable from LightGBM, gradient boosting, logistic regression or the MLP; it is better than the random forest, the SVM and the decision tree. Its AUC advantage over every other model is significant but small (0.02 or less, except over the decision tree).

Averaged over the eight models, moving from the binary to the three-class setting lowers macro F1 by \gapf{} (\gapRelf\% of the binary value), accuracy by \gapaccuracy{} and macro AUC by \gapauc. For XGBoost, class-wise F1 is \multiclassXGBoostfdropout{} for Dropout and \multiclassXGBoostfgraduate{} for Graduate but only \multiclassXGBoostfenrolled{} for Enrolled. The confusion matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}) shows that \cmEnrolledCorrect\% of delayed students are recognised, \cmEnrolledAsGrad\% are predicted to graduate and \cmEnrolledAsDrop\% to drop out. Delayed students sit between the two terminal groups in feature space, and as noted in Section~\ref{sec:data} the class itself mixes future graduates and future dropouts.

Dropout detection is much less affected. The probability that the three-class XGBoost model assigns to Dropout ranks dropouts against all other students with an AUC of \multiclassXGBoostaucdropout. This is lower than the binary AUC (\binaryXGBoostaucdropout), but the two numbers answer different questions. The binary model never has to tell dropouts apart from delayed students, the hardest cases, so its figure overstates how well a deployed model would do on a full cohort.

\subsection{Prediction at enrolment and after the first semester (RQ3)}

Table~\ref{tab:stages} and Figure~\ref{fig:perclass} follow the three-class problem through time. With enrolment data only, the best macro F1 is \stageEnrolF{} and the Dropout AUC \stageEnrolAucDrop. Adding first-semester records raises these to \stageSemoneF{} and \stageSemoneAucDrop; adding the second semester gives \stageFullF{} and \stageFullAucDrop. XGBoost keeps \retainsem\% of its full macro F1 with first-semester data and \retainenrol\% with enrolment data only. Most of the information arrives with the first semester. The delayed class benefits most from the second semester (F1 from \stageSemoneFenrolled{} to \stageFullFenrolled), which fits the idea that delay builds up through units that are not passed in the second semester. A first-semester model is good enough to start interventions a semester earlier. A model at enrolment ranks dropout risk only moderately well (AUC \stageEnrolAucDrop), so it suits low-cost support such as a welcome call or a mentor.

\subsection{Subgroups (RQ4)}

Table~\ref{tab:subgroups} and Figure~\ref{fig:subgroups} report errors by group. Among scholarship holders, whose dropout rate is \subScholarshipYesRate, the model identifies \subScholarshipYesTpr{} of the dropouts, against \subScholarshipNoTpr{} among other students (dropout rate \subScholarshipNoRate). Students who enrolled at 20 or younger (dropout rate \subAgeatenrolmentletwentyRate) have a recall of \subAgeatenrolmentletwentyTpr, compared with \subAgeatenrolmentgttwentyfiveTpr{} for students older than 25 (dropout rate \subAgeatenrolmentgttwentyfiveRate). Male students, whose dropout rate is higher (\subGenderMaleRate{} vs.\ \subGenderFemaleRate), receive more false alerts (\subGenderMaleFpr{} vs.\ \subGenderFemaleFpr) and also have higher recall.

In each case the model is more sensitive in the group where dropout is common and misses more dropouts where it is rare. A likely reason is that group membership, or variables correlated with it, carries part of the signal. An alert list built from a single threshold will under-represent dropouts from low-risk groups, such as young full-time students and scholarship holders. Group-specific thresholds or a review of the missed cases are possible remedies; which is appropriate depends on the institution's goals \citep{kizilcec2022,baker2022}.

\subsection{Alert lists and thresholds (RQ5)}

Figure~\ref{fig:alerts} and Table~\ref{tab:alerts} show what happens when the scores are used to choose whom to contact. After the first semester, \alertSemonePPctTen{} of the students in the top 10\% of the ranking later drop out, \alertSemoneLiftTen{} times the base rate of \baseRate. This list reaches \alertSemoneRPctTen{} of all dropouts, close to the maximum possible, since a list of that size can hold at most \perfectReachTen{} of them. A list of the top 20\% reaches \alertSemoneRPctTwenty{} of dropouts, and \alertSemonePPctTwenty{} of the students on it drop out. Adding the second semester changes these figures little (\alertFullRPctTwenty{} reached at 20\%), so for list-based advising most of the value is available one semester earlier. At enrolment, a 20\% list reaches \alertEnrolRPctTwenty{} of dropouts (precision \alertEnrolPPctTwenty), or \alertEnrolNofeeRPctTwenty{} (precision \alertEnrolNofeePPctTwenty) without the fee-status variables.

In the first-semester model, \profileToptenZeroApproved\% of the top 10\% passed no first-semester unit at all (against \profileAllZeroApproved\% of all students) and \profileToptenFeesNotUpToDate\% were behind on fees. An adviser looking at grades and payments would find most of these students without a model. In the next 10\% of the ranking only \profileNexttenZeroApproved\% passed no unit, and precision is lower.

\paragraph{Feature selection.} With selection performed inside cross-validation, no reduced feature set improved on all 36 features (Figure~\ref{fig:fs}; macro F1 \fsFull). \subsection{Transfer to unseen programmes}
\label{sec:transfer}

Within a programme the model never saw, the ranking of students is almost unchanged (Figure~\ref{fig:transfer}). After the first semester, the median change in Dropout AUC across the \trNProg{} programmes with at least 50 students is $\trSemoneXGBoostMedDrop$ for XGBoost and $\trSemoneLogisticRegressionMedDrop$ for logistic regression, and the held-out AUC ranges from \trSemoneXGBoostOutAucMin{} to \trSemoneXGBoostOutAucMax. Pooled over all students, the Dropout AUC falls from \trSemoneXGBoostInAuc{} to \trSemoneXGBoostOutAuc{} for XGBoost and from \trSemoneLogisticRegressionInAuc{} to \trSemoneLogisticRegressionOutAuc{} for logistic regression (Table~\ref{tab:transfer}), so here the simpler model transfers better. Section~\ref{sec:upv} shows that this does not carry over to the second university.

Most of the pooled loss comes from the overall level of dropout in a new programme, which the model misjudges. Its mean predicted dropout probability differs from the observed rate by \trSemoneXGBoostCalGap{} on average (\trSemoneLogisticRegressionCalGap{} for logistic regression). Ranking within the programme survives, but the probabilities need recalibrating to the new programme's base rate. In Animation and Multimedia Design, \trZeroAnim\% of students have no curricular units recorded in either semester, including \trZeroGradAnim\% of those who graduated; no other programme has any such records. When the programme is part of the training data, its own students teach the model that these zeros do not signal failure, and when it is held out the zeros are read as failure. It then predicts a mean dropout probability of \trAnimMeanP{} against an observed \trAnimRate{}, and the AUC falls from \trAnimInAuc{} to \trAnimOutAuc. Without this programme, the pooled AUC of XGBoost goes from \trSemoneXGBoostInAucExcl{} to \trSemoneXGBoostOutAucExcl. The same pattern holds after the second semester.

A model trained on other programmes can rank students in a new one. Its probabilities should be recalibrated on that programme's first cohort, and each programme's data recording should be checked before use.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the Enrolled class is.} Reading Enrolled as delayed completion changes the interpretation of the three-class problem. The low F1 for this class means that delay is hard to separate from both dropout and graduation with the available variables, partly because the class contains students on both paths. For an institution, dropout and delay are separate targets. Dropout reaches an AUC of \multiclassXGBoostaucdropout, the delayed class an F1 of only \multiclassXGBoostfenrolled, and delay may need more frequent data, such as term-by-term progress, than two semester summaries provide.

\paragraph{What the replication shows.} The second university differs in country, in how it defines dropout, in its dropout rate (\upvRatePct\% against 32\%) and in the variables it records. The results that hold in both places concern evaluation and use. Models tie, probabilities are calibrated, a first-semester model ranks dropouts well enough to build a short list, and a single threshold misses more dropouts where dropout is rare. Results that depend on the structure of one dataset did not carry over, among them the better transfer of logistic regression and the share of the first-year signal that arrives in the first semester (\repShareUci\% at UCI, \repShareUpv\% at UPV). The delayed class could not be tested, because the second dataset does not record delay.

\paragraph{Which model.} In three-class macro F1, 5 of the 8 models are statistically tied, in the binary setting 7 of 8, and at the second university \upvSemoneTied{} of 8 in Dropout AUC. Logistic regression is among the tied models in both, has calibrated probabilities and is easy to explain to advisers. Boosted trees give small but consistent AUC gains. With accuracy this close, interpretability and ease of maintenance can decide between them. Tree ensembles are usually strong on tabular data \citep{grinsztajn2022}, yet here their margin over a well-specified linear model is small. When the data shift to a new cohort or degree, neither kind of model was consistently ahead.

\paragraph{Using the scores.} For an institution, the most useful output is a ranked list of students. After one semester, a list covering a fifth of the cohort reaches more than half of eventual dropouts with few false alerts, and calibrated probabilities make it possible to choose the list size from the expected number of dropouts. Because a single threshold under-serves groups where dropout is rare, the list should be checked by group, and group-specific thresholds are a cheap correction where equal recall is the goal. At UPV the same correction equalised recall mostly by flagging fewer part-time students, whose false-alert rate fell from \upvThrYearParttimeSingleFpr{} to \upvThrYearParttimeGroupFpr. Accuracy on a new cohort was lower at UPV already for the 2021 entrants, so a deployed model should be refitted as each cohort finishes its first year and recalibrated on a new programme's first term.

\paragraph{Reporting.} Three practices would make results on this benchmark easier to compare: reporting class-wise scores alongside macro averages, using repeated cross-validation with corrected tests instead of one split, and performing feature selection and resampling inside the training folds.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The UCI data come from one institution and cover cohorts up to 2018/19. The second university confirms several of the dropout results, but it records no delay, so the three-class findings, the main subject of this paper, rest on one institution. The two datasets define dropout differently and hold different variables, so we compare patterns, and the absolute values are not comparable. Labels for the 2022 UPV entrants may be less complete than for earlier years. A test of the three-class results at an institution that records delayed completion is the most important next step. The recording time of the fee-status variables is not documented. We report results with and without them, and the enrolment-stage results depend on this choice. We did not use resampling methods such as SMOTE, which can raise recall for the delayed class at the cost of precision and calibration. The subgroup analysis uses three attributes and does not consider intersections. Finally, the predictors are semester summaries, and finer-grained data could help with the delayed class in particular.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On the UCI dropout benchmark, binary results overstate how well a deployed model separates students, most model rankings are within noise, and the main difficulty of the three-class problem is identifying delayed students. Dropouts can be ranked well after one semester (AUC \stageSemoneAucDrop), with calibrated probabilities. A list covering a fifth of the cohort then reaches more than half of them. Where dropout is rare, recall is lower, and group-specific thresholds close the gap for a few extra false alerts. We found the same ties and the same recall gap at a Spanish university that defines dropout differently. There, a model trained on one cohort lost accuracy on the next, and neither logistic regression nor XGBoost transferred consistently better.
